How to make a tattoo stencil from a photo or drawing

Last updated: November 25, 2026

The short answer

To make a tattoo stencil from a photo or drawing, load a clear image into a tattoo stencil maker, choose Drawing or Photo mode, adjust sensitivity, smoothing and line weight until only the lines you want remain, set the width in millimetres, then download the PNG and print it at true size.

What a tattoo stencil generator actually does

A stencil is the outline of a design, reduced to clean lines so it can be transferred to skin. A tattoo stencil generator takes a picture and keeps the edges that matter while dropping tone, paper texture and noise. The result is dark lines on a white background, which is the form most transfer methods need.

Skinstencil does this with ordinary image processing in your browser. Your file is read on your own device and is not uploaded. That matters when a design belongs to a client. It also means the tool works as soon as the page has loaded, with no account.

Choose a source that will convert cleanly

Good sources share a few traits. Use one that matches as many of these as you can:

  • Clear contrast between the lines and the background, such as ink on white paper.
  • The design fills the frame instead of sitting small in a corner.
  • Flat and square to the camera or scanner, with no curl or perspective.
  • Even light with no shadow of your hand or phone across the page.
  • Enough resolution that thin lines are still several pixels wide.

A flatbed scan is the easiest way to get all of that. If you photograph instead, work near a window in daylight, hold the phone parallel to the page, and avoid the flash, which leaves a bright patch in the middle. Only use designs you made or have the right to use.

Drawing mode or Photo mode

The tool asks what kind of source you are loading, because a line drawing and a photograph need different treatment.

Drawing

Use Drawing for sketches, flash, lettering and anything already made of lines. Dark marks on a light page are the design, and the aim is to keep them close to how you drew them.

Photo

Use Photo for pictures with soft shading, such as a portrait or an animal. A photo has no outline to keep, so edges where tone changes have to stand in for lines. Expect to spend more time on the sliders with photos, and expect to decide which details you want to draw by hand.

How to make a tattoo stencil online, step by step

These are the steps in order. The sections below explain the controls in more detail.

  1. Choose a clear source. Pick the drawing or photo with the best contrast and the least clutter. If you have two versions, use the one drawn with a darker, more even pen.
  2. Scan or photograph it in even light. Scan flat if you can. Otherwise photograph in daylight, parallel to the page, with the design filling the frame and no shadows or flash.
  3. Load it into the tool. Open the free tool and drop in a JPG, PNG or WebP file, or click to choose one. It is processed in your browser and not uploaded.
  4. Pick Drawing or Photo mode. Choose Drawing for line art and flash, Photo for pictures with soft shading.
  5. Adjust sensitivity. Sensitivity decides how faint a mark can be and still become a line. Lower it if shading turns into lines. Raise it if thin lines are missing.
  6. Adjust smoothing and line weight. Smoothing rounds off jagged pixel edges. Line weight makes lines thicker or thinner. If lines look faint when printed, a heavier line weight usually helps.
  7. Remove specks. Raise Remove specks to clear small dots from paper grain or dust until only deliberate marks are left.
  8. Check what was lost with the drift check. Turn on the drift check. Lost lines show in red and added lines in blue, so you can see what your settings changed against a fixed, sensitive reading of your original.
  9. Set the width in millimetres. Enter the width you want on skin. The tool shows the matching size at 300 dpi and a true-size ruler.
  10. Download or print at true size. Download the PNG and print it so the printout measures the width you set. Check it against a ruler before it goes near anyone.

Using the controls without losing the design

Treat the sliders as a conversation rather than a recipe. Change one, look, and change it back if it hurt more than it helped.

  • Sensitivity is the biggest lever. Too high and every smudge becomes a line. Too low and fine detail disappears.
  • Smoothing helps most on scans of small drawings, where lines look stair-stepped when enlarged. Too much smoothing rounds off sharp corners and small gaps.
  • Line weight is a matter of taste and of your transfer method. Thin hairlines can disappear when printed or transferred, so test on paper first.
  • Remove specks is the cleanup control. Raise it until the dust goes, and stop before it starts removing dots that belong to the design, such as stippling.

Stippling and tiny dotwork are the usual casualties. If your design depends on them, keep specks low and check the result closely.

Why the drift check matters

A stencil that looks clean can still be missing something. Dropping a fine line is easy when you are chasing a tidy result, and easy to miss on a small screen.

The drift check compares the stencil with the lines found in your original. Red marks a line that was lost. Blue marks a line that was added. The reference is a fixed, sensitive reading of the original, so lowering sensitivity or raising Remove specks will show red on purpose. Red is not always a mistake, but it is always worth a look before you commit to a size.

Why are some lines missing from my stencil?

Sensitivity may be too low, or Remove specks too high, so faint lines were treated as noise. Turn on the drift check: lost lines show in red. Raise sensitivity or lower Remove specks and check again.
