Tattoo size chart: typical sizes by body part

Last updated: November 26, 2026

The short answer

A tattoo size chart lists the typical width or length that suits each body part, for example 2 to 5 cm (about 1 to 2 inches) at the wrist and 8 to 25 cm (3 to 10 inches) along the forearm. These are guides, not rules. Print your design at true size and hold it on your skin to check.

Tattoo size chart by body part

A tattoo size chart gives you a starting point before you talk to an artist. The ranges below are typical sizes for the longest side of a single design on an average adult. They are guidance, not rules. Bodies differ, and so do designs.

Read the numbers as the length of the design along the limb, or the height on a flat area like the back. A forearm piece can run the full length of the arm, while a wrist piece is usually a small band or symbol.

How to read the chart

The ranges are wide on purpose. A 10 cm (4 inch) rose is a small tattoo on a thigh and a large one behind the ear. What matters is how the design relates to the space around it.

  • Small pieces sit well on the wrist, ankle, hand, neck and behind the ear.
  • Medium pieces suit the upper arm, forearm, calf, shoulder blade and ribs.
  • Large pieces use the back, chest and thigh, where there is room for a design to breathe.

If your idea falls outside the range for the spot you like, consider a different placement or a different size. Wrapping a design around a limb, for example, lets a long design fit where a flat one would not.

Minimum size for detail

Size is not only about looks. Tattoo ink spreads slightly in the skin over the years, so tiny details can blur. The smaller the design, the fewer details it can hold and still stay readable.

Fine line work

Fine line designs use thin, light strokes. They look delicate, but they need enough space between lines. Many artists suggest keeping a design at least a few centimetres across and avoiding tiny text or crowded patterns. Ask your artist for the smallest size they are happy to do for your design.

Bold work

Bold designs with thick outlines and solid fill hold up at smaller sizes because the shapes are simple and the gaps between them are wide. A bold symbol can work at a size where the same image in fine line would turn to mush.

When you shrink a design, simplify it. Remove small background elements, widen tight gaps and make text larger than you think you need.

How to measure on your body

You can measure your own placement in a few minutes with a soft tape measure or a ruler. A soft tape follows curves, which a rigid ruler does not.

  1. Mark the top and bottom of the spot you have in mind with a washable pen, or with small pieces of tape.
  2. Measure the straight distance between the marks, then measure again along the curve of the body.
  3. Measure the width at the widest point of the area.
  4. Write down both numbers in centimetres and inches, so you can compare them with the chart.
  5. Leave a margin of at least a finger width on each side so the design does not sit right at an edge.

The straight measurement and the curved one differ more on round areas like the upper arm and calf. A design that is 10 cm wide on paper will look narrower once it wraps around a curve, because part of it turns away from the viewer.

How placement and skin change the size you need

The same design can look different depending on where it sits and whose skin it is on.

  • Curves. A design on a rounded area appears smaller and slightly distorted from the front. On the upper arm or calf you may want to go a bit larger than a flat drawing suggests.
  • Movement. Skin near joints stretches and folds. Designs there are often kept smaller or simpler, and your artist may advise on the exact position.
  • Body size. A 15 cm design sits differently on a slim forearm and a broad one. Judge it on your own body, not on a photo of someone else's.
  • Skin tone. Contrast changes how a design reads, so ask your artist what shows up best on your skin.
  • Ageing. Lines soften with time. Giving a design some room helps it stay clear.

Final checks before you book

  • Does the printed stencil look right when you stand, sit and move?
  • Is there enough space between lines for the style you chose?
  • Is any text large enough to read from a normal distance?

Your artist has the final say on size and detail. Bring your printout and the measurements, and treat the chart as the start of the conversation.

Questions

What size is a small tattoo?

There is no fixed rule, but many people call a design under about 5 cm (2 inches) small. Placements like the wrist, ankle and behind the ear usually suit this size.

How big is a medium tattoo?

Medium usually means roughly 8 to 15 cm (3 to 6 inches) on the longest side. It fits well on a forearm, upper arm, calf or shoulder blade.

What is the smallest size a tattoo can be?

It depends on the style and the artist. Fine detail needs more room than bold shapes, so ask your artist for the minimum size that will stay clear for your design.

How do I find out how big my tattoo will be on my body?

Print the design at true size and hold it on the spot, or tape it there. You can then judge the size in a mirror and while you move.

Are tattoo sizes the same for everyone?

No. The ranges in a chart are typical, and your body shape, skin and the design itself all change what looks right.
